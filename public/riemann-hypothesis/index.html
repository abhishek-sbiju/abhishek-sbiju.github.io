The Riemann
Hypothesis.

Every nontrivial zero of ζ(s) has real part ½. Nobody has proved it. This is the road to understanding it: seven stages, why each one matters, when to move on, and the free PDFs for each.

The road

00

Proofs

Why

Every book after this one is written in proofs. Until you can read and write one, you are only looking at the pages. Coming from code, this is the real gate: a compiler checks your code, but only you can check a proof.

Move on when

You can prove √2 is irrational and that there are infinitely many primes with the book closed, and point to the exact line where a wrong proof breaks.

01

Elementary number theory

Why

The primes live here before any analysis touches them. Congruences, unique factorisation, and functions like φ and μ all come back later, inside ζ(s).

Move on when

You can prove Fermat's little theorem and the Chinese remainder theorem, use Möbius inversion, and show why every prime of the form 4k + 1 is a sum of two squares.

02

Real analysis

Why

ζ(s) is defined as an infinite sum, 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ⋯. Limits, series and uniform convergence are what make a sum like that mean anything. Skip this, and every later proof turns into something you just have to trust.

Move on when

You can prove that ∑ 1/n² converges while ∑ 1/n does not, and explain why a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.

03

Complex analysis

Why

The sum for ζ(s) only works when Re(s) > 1, and every zero the hypothesis is about lies where that sum blows up. Analytic continuation is the only way to get there. Contour integrals are also how the primes finally get counted.

Move on when

You can state Cauchy's theorem, evaluate an integral with residues, and explain analytic continuation to a friend without writing a single symbol.

04

Analytic number theory

Why

This is where analysis starts counting primes: Chebyshev's bounds, Dirichlet's theorem, and then the prime number theorem, π(x) ~ x / log x. That is the exact result the Riemann hypothesis would sharpen.

Move on when

You can prove the prime number theorem start to finish (Newman's four-page route counts), and explain why π(106) = 78,498 sits closer to li(106) ≈ 78,628 than to 106/log 106 ≈ 72,382.

05

The zeta function

Why

Now the real object. Riemann's own paper, the functional equation, the explicit formula that writes the primes as a sum over the zeros, and the zero-free regions. This is where you finally see what the hypothesis says, and why so much of mathematics depends on it.

Move on when

You can derive the functional equation, explain why RH is the same statement as π(x) = li(x) + O(√x log x), and follow Hardy's 1914 proof that infinitely many zeros lie on the line.

06

The frontier

Why

Read what people are working on now, and try small problems of your own. The goal isn't to prove RH. It's to be able to follow the people who are trying. Linear algebra comes back here, because the zeros are spaced like the eigenvalues of large random matrices.

Move on when

You don't. This is the stage you keep for life.
